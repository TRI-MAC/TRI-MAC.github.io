The Feedback Loops We Don't See

Every organization is a control system — most just don't know what they're regulating.

◎
TRI-MAC Engineering

In 1956, W. Ross Ashby published An Introduction to Cybernetics, a book that would quietly reshape how we think about control, regulation, and communication in complex systems. At its heart was a deceptively simple principle: the Law of Requisite Variety.

"Only variety can absorb variety." — W. Ross Ashby

The idea is this: if a system faces N possible disturbances, it needs at least N possible responses to maintain stability. A thermostat works because it has two states (heat on, heat off) to match the two states of the room (too cold, warm enough). But what happens when the disturbances are organizational? When they're cultural? When they're the invisible dynamics of a team?

The Hidden Regulators

Every team has feedback loops operating below the surface. Some are obvious — sprint retrospectives, code reviews, standups. But the most powerful ones are informal:

  • Social signaling — who speaks first in meetings shapes what gets discussed
  • Implicit prioritization — the bugs that get fixed fastest reveal what the team actually values
  • Attention allocation — where senior engineers spend their time creates gravitational pull

These loops aren't designed. They emerge. And because they're emergent, they're often invisible to the people inside them.

Seeing the System

The first step toward better regulation is observation. Not performance metrics — those are outputs, not loops. Instead, look for circular causality: 

A influences B → B influences C → C influences A
       ↑                                    │
       └────────── feedback ────────────────┘

When you find these cycles, you've found the actual control structure of your organization. It may look nothing like the org chart.

Designing for Variety

Once you can see the loops, you can ask: does this system have requisite variety? Are there enough response mechanisms to handle the disturbances it faces?

Most teams fail this test. They have one escalation path, one deployment process, one way to handle incidents. When a novel situation arises — and in complex systems, novelty is guaranteed — the system breaks down not because of incompetence, but because of insufficient variety.

◆
Key insight: Adding variety doesn't mean adding complexity. It means expanding the range of responses available at the point where disturbances meet the system.

This is why diverse teams outperform homogeneous ones — not just ethically, but cybernetically. A team with varied backgrounds, perspectives, and problem-solving approaches has more variety available to match the variety of challenges it faces.

Practical Steps

How do you increase requisite variety in practice?

  1. Map your feedback loops — both formal and informal. Draw them. Make them visible.
  2. Identify variety bottlenecks — where does the system have only one response to many possible disturbances?
  3. Add response mechanisms — not rules, but capabilities. Can the team handle X if it happens?
  4. Shorten loop times — faster feedback means faster adaptation. Reduce the delay between signal and response.

The organizations that thrive in complexity aren't the ones with the best plans. They're the ones with the most variety in their responses — the ones that can match whatever the environment throws at them.

Ashby figured this out seventy years ago. We're still catching up.
